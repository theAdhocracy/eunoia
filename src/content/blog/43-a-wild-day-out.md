As most people who know me can tell you, it doesn’t take much persuasion to get me into a zoo. However from the moment I step through their gates I find myself constantly comparing them to what is still my all time favourite: Tenerife’s Lorro Parque. As such Singapore Zoo had a lot to live up to but I have to say it performed very well.

The park is situated almost central on the island of Singapore, alongside its sister attraction, Night Safari, that we also visited. If you combine the two, which can be done quite easily as the Night Safari opens just as the Zoo itself is closing, then you definitely have a very different and unique experience. The Night Safari itself is a pretty clever idea; most animals in the wild are active at dusk/dawn and on top of that are the further thousands that are purely nocturnal. Most zoos struggle with this, creating expensive “Night Buildings” which still rarely seem to work. Singapore goes one better and just allows you to view the animals when they are naturally active, meaning a much higher success rate and also a system in which particularly rare animals seem to breed much more happily.

We began, though, with the Zoo proper and I must say first impressions were extremely high. Singapore zoo prides itself on two aspects: easy viewing of animals and the aesthetics of the zoo in total. As such having the first exhibit you enter (after meeting a few free roaming Cotton-Top Tamarins) is completely fantastic; they successfully recreate an entire riverside habitat from southern Asia allowing them to mix in False Gharials and a number of small bird and deer species that can all live happily, as well as a group of rare Siamang gibbons that are happily and without restriction swinging around above your heads. In such they set the tone: no exhibit only contains one animal, they often use natural barriers rather than manmade to “fence” the animals into an area and most exhibits are multi-storey, so you can look at the ground and see one species and look to the canopy and see another living in complete harmony.

I was also impressed with the variety of endangered species in the zoo as well as the management’s ability to induce breeding in some very rare and normally fussy animals such as the Pygmy Hippo. I wasn’t even aware these relatively newly discovered animals were even in captivity so I was very surprised to see three adults and one very healthy offspring. On top of this were a whole host of exceptional exhibits ranging from the beautiful white tigers with its entire waterfall and multi-level cascade to the recreated Ethiopian highlands home to the Hamadryas baboons, another animal highly threatened that I hadn’t known was held in captivity. The zoo also went out of its way to show you a cultural side to those animals it could, so as you were admiring the antics of the baboons you found yourself in recreated rondavals and mud-walled huts with tribesmen carvings and even a small section describing the tools used to irrigate, terrace and tend the steep terrain of Ethiopia.

Free-roaming exhibits were also plentiful, most notably the Orang Utans and the Jungle Fights Back biodome which allowed you to walk amongst a large collection of jungle birds as well as stranger creatures such as ring-tailed lemurs, tree kangaroos and even two-toed sloths! The Orang exhibit was so free roaming we didn’t even realise we had entered it until we noticed one of the huge apes swinging gracefully by overhead and the canopy boardwalk provided allowed access to the Man of the Forest rarely seen outside of Borneo itself.

Not everything was perfect for the zoo however; its shows looked entertaining and very different, which was nice to see, however a single showing per day meant we missed them both. The layout could have used some improvement as although there were regular signposts zoo maps were scarce and the tracks often interlinked in ways unexpected meaning we spent far too long backtracking or hitting dead ends. Finally, and probably most notably, although many of the newer exhibits were world class as you travelled through the zoo you couldn’t help notice some areas were in need of attention, unfortunately the Big Cats section being the worst. Perhaps rather than investing funds in their latest Outback Adventure section which was rather boring and tame in comparison to other areas, they should have created some new housing for their Pumas, Ocelots and Leopards all of which were pacing back and forth around their rather tiny habitats. The lions, of course, had it a little better but still appeared to have very little in the way of stimulus.

That said we both left the zoo feeling we had spent our time and money well; the newer areas definitely set a standard few other zoos could achieve and even my beloved Lorro Parque has its parts in need of attention. The overall exhibit design and general layout really did mean Singapore Zoo deserves its title of the Jungle Garden.
